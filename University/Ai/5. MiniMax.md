Games vs. Search

Games have "unpredictable" opponent => Solution is a contingency plan
Moves have time limits => Unlikely to find optimal goal, must approx

Game Properties:
- No. of players
- Time limitations
- Modelling our adversary, can we just consider the game state or do we need to consider other player's strategy (hence look at previous moves in game)
<h4>AI vs. Game Theory</h4>
AI:
AI tends to look at game playing as an elaboration of the search problem, achieve a goal 

Game strategies are contingent plans aimed at achieving a goal within the context of a reactive and opposing environment 

Sometimes called **combinatorial game theory**

(Simultaneous) Game Theory:

Typically reduces games to a situation where players simultaneously choose actions from a set of choices; each player gains some reward or pays some penalty depending on the combination of actions that were chosen by them & by other players.
<h4>Game Strategy</h4>
Simple way of playing a game

Mathematically, game strategy can be modelled by a function which determines the next move of a player for any state of the game that might occur when it's that player's move 

Strategy is associated with only one player of the game, doesn't determine the moves of other players

For computerised game play, would typically define a strategy by means of some kind of rule set and/or algorithm

<u>Good Strategy</u>
A strategy for a game doesn't necessarily have to be a good way of playing it. Whether a strategy is good (likely to lead to a win) may depend on what strategy is being used by the opponent(s)

Some strategies may be good against certain opposing strategies but bad against others

<u>Winning Strategy</u>
One that will always win the game whatever moves the opponent(s) play.

Sometimes could be a winning strategy from the beginning or winning strategy may appear from a game state that occurs part way through the game

<h4>Minimax</h4>
AI moves to a position that gives the best achievable payoff assuming the opponent always makes their best play (even if they don't)

Need to look at subsequent moves

Can define a recursive procedure for calculating the value of a move based on the evaluation of subsequent moves 

Since moves alternate between our player and the opponent, calculation alternates between taking the maximum and the minimum of the values calculated for the following state
<h4>Game-State Evaluation Function</h4>
Game-state evaluation used in Minimax is normally a function such that a high value is good for the agent using minimax, low is good for the opponent. 

States are always evaluated with respect to the same player (the 'Max' player), hence when opponent 'Min player' takes a turn they always look for a move giving the lowest value of that function which is good for them (bad for 'Max')

**You want to <u>maximise</u> your score (win as much as possible) while opponent wants to <u>minimise</u> your score (trying to beat you)**

The function can be based on actual points scored, can be a heuristic that estimates the winning potential of the state. In many games we can evaluate the state for each player separately and then estimate the winning potential for 'Max' as:
$$w(s) = v_{max\_player}(s) - v_{min\_player}(s)$$

<h4>Minimax Algorithm</h4>
![[Pasted image 20250517221145.png]]

Properties:
- Complete - Yes if tree is finite
- Optimal - Yes against an optimal opponent
- Time Complexity - O(b<sup>m</sup>)
- Space Complexity - O(m) (using depth-first exploration)

For chess: $$b \approx 35, m \approx 100$$ for "reasonable" games => exact solution completely infeasible
b = branching factor, m = depth
<h4>Limited Search</h4>
Suppose we have 100 seconds to explore 10<sup>4</sup> nodes/sec => 10<sup>6</sup> nodes/move

But 35<sup>4</sup> = 1500625 (> 10<sup>6</sup>)
So can only do around 4 ply look-ahead which is quite naive play 

ply = one player's move
full move = move by each player (2 plies in 2-player game)

Standard approach:
- Cut-off test (e.g depth limit, perhaps add quiescence test)
- Evaluation function (gives estimated desirability of position, heuristic)
![[Pasted image 20250517223214.png]]
For chess, typically linear weighted sum of features:
$$Eval(s) = w_{1}f_{1}(s) + w_{2}f_{2}(s) + \dots + w_{n}f_{n}(s)$$
e.g. $w_{1} = 9$ with $f_{1}(s) = \text{(number of white queens)} - \text{(number of black queens)}$

<h4>Cutting off search</h4>
Depth limit, stops the tree from expanding

Inherently danger in stopping lookahead at some limited depth. Something bad may happen shortly after that depth

This is more likely if the game is in a phase where things are changing fast (e.g. multi-piece trade-off in chess)

<h4>Quiescence</h4>
When game is in a phase of play where the available reasonable moves only make small differences to the strength of either players position, game is said to be **quiescent**

Performance of lookahead minimax may be significantly improved by using some measure of quiescence to vary the depth of lookahead accordingly

However, the **horizon problem** may cause series problems for some kind of game, can make quiescence misleading. Problem arises when some bad consequence that will happen after a long sequence of uneventful moves 

The **Horizon Problem** is when a search algorithm fails to see consequences of important moves or events just beyond the fixed search depth, thus makes poor evaluation of current position.

<h4>Limitations of minimax</h4>
Doesn't take account of fact that opponent may not play as expected (may use different evaluations or make some mistakes)

With depth limited MiniMax, only as good as game state evaluation heuristic. May be a crude measure for the more subtle games. 

Will not perform well when there are many choices that lead to only slightly different states that can't easily be differentiated by heuristics (e.g. slow developing games like Go)
